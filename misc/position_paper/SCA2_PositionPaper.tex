\section*{Statement of Significance}

Economics has always studied latent constructs---preferences, trust, patience, social preferences---but its instruments for observing them are costly, sparse, and each is an imperfect representation of the underlying attribute. Which survey question you use to measure ``trust'' can change the answer you get about how trust relates to development as much as the phenomenon itself does. As AI makes candidate measures cheap, the binding constraint is no longer building measures; it is knowing which ones are scientifically admissible. This paper develops two open-source instruments: (i)~a pipeline that instantiates a measured cultural state in a language-model policy, producing low-cost synthetic populations for experimental and comparative-statical analysis; and (ii)~a partial-identification tool (\texttt{cvprofiles}) that, given a researcher's nomological network, selects the set of admissible measures of a latent construct and reports the range of any downstream estimate consistent with all admissible measures. Applied to the Global Preferences Survey and the World Values Survey, the framework shows that only a minority of candidate survey measures are admissible representations of the six GPS preference dimensions, and that the canonical single-item trust measure and a multi-item composite agree on average trust but disagree on \emph{who} trusts---with direct consequences for hypothesis generation, pre-field survey design, and cross-country policy analysis.

\section*{Abstract}

Large language models are increasingly used as simulators of human judgment in economics, yet their default alignment reflects predominantly Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, and Democratic (\WEIRD{}) preference data. Earlier Synthetic Cultural Agents (\SCA{}~1.0) conditioned models on ethnographic profiles at inference time---useful for piloting but reduced-form: cultural signal lives in the prompt, vanishes with the session, and supports no formal identification. \SCA{}~2.0 encodes culture structurally instead. A six-dimensional Global Preferences Survey (\GPS{}) state vector---standardized deviations from the global mean---generates culture-conditioned synthetic preference pairs; Direct Preference Optimization (\DPO{}) fine-tunes an open-weight student model on these labels, so dispositions live in the weights, not the prompt; and the resulting agents are evaluated out-of-sample against human survey responses. Because the target country is never named, the design pre-empts stereotype leakage by construction---an open pipeline for synthetic experiments on cultural states.

The second contribution is a measurement instrument. We formalize the Cronbach--Meehl nomological network as a partial-identification problem over a finite menu of candidate measures and implement it in the open-source package \cvp{} (score, restrict, identify, report): given a researcher-supplied network of literature-anchored moment restrictions, it returns the admissible set $M^*$ and the construct-identified range $[L,U]$ of any downstream target. On the \GPS{}/\WVS{} trust bridge, three of four WVS trust facets are admissible representations of GPS trust, while the canonical single item (Q57) is rejected at the stated threshold; a ``survivor composite'' reproduces GPS trust's demographic structure where the single item does not---representation choice is consequential, not cosmetic. Empirically, the Mexico adapter matches its target population better than the cross adapter on 97.1\% of 35 items ($\Delta\text{TVD}=0.1495$, 95\% CI $[0.0964, 0.2152]$); the USA adapter degrades relative to the base model, mechanism unresolved.

\noindent\textit{Keywords:} cultural preferences; Direct Preference Optimization; Bradley--Terry; Global Preferences Survey; structural estimation; construct validity; partial identification; World Values Survey

\section{Introduction}

Social scientists increasingly use large language models as low-cost simulators of survey responses, experimental subjects, and policy interlocutors \cite{horton2023,achar2023,argyle2023}. The practical appeal is clear: a model can generate thousands of responses in minutes, enabling exploratory design before expensive fieldwork. The scientific risk is equally clear. Preference fine-tuning and human feedback data for frontier models are concentrated in \WEIRD{} populations \cite{henrich2010,falk2018}. When a model is asked to ``answer as someone in Mexico City,'' its behavior still largely reflects that default alignment---unless culture is introduced in a disciplined way.

The first generation of Synthetic Cultural Agents \cite{capra2025} showed that persona prompting can reproduce non-\WEIRD{} patterns in economic experiments to a surprising degree. For piloting and hypothesis generation, that result is valuable. Methodologically, however, persona prompting is a reduced-form intervention: cultural information lives in the context window, vanishes when the session ends, and provides no structural object (preferences, technology, information) that can be identified, tested, or counterfactually varied.

\SCA{}~2.0 reframes the problem as \emph{structural preference estimation} under \emph{measurement discipline}. The core conjecture is:

\begin{quote}
\textit{Cultural preferences measured by the Global Preferences Survey can be encoded into language-model policies via preference fine-tuning, so that implied utilities recover the direction of culture-conditioned choices and support formal evaluation against independent survey moments.}
\end{quote}

The design rests on a sharp distinction between \emph{direct} and \emph{indirect} cultural information. Persona prompting---and ethnographic prompting more broadly---supplies cultural information \emph{directly} at inference time: the model is told who it is (``answer as someone in Mexico City''), and it plays along with the frame. \SCA{}~2.0 supplies it \emph{indirectly}, through the training labels alone. The model is never told which population it represents; its cultural disposition must be inferred from the pattern of which responses were preferred in its training data. This has a practical payoff that is often overlooked: because the target country is never mentioned, the pipeline pre-empts stereotype leakage---the model cannot lean on country-name stereotypes because it is not given the name---and the same design transfers to any population description that can be encoded as a preference vector.

The paper makes two contributions, which are deliberately coupled. The \textbf{first} is an open-source pipeline for \emph{synthetic experiments on cultural states}: GPS-grounded synthetic preference pairs, DPO fine-tuning of an open-weight student model with unconditioned prompts, and out-of-sample evaluation on human survey responses. Because the cultural state enters only through the training labels, an analyst can hold the prompt design fixed while varying the state---a controlled intervention on the training distribution, not a randomized treatment of human culture. The \textbf{second} is a measurement instrument: since the scientific status of the enterprise hinges on whether our measures (survey facets, composites, adapters) actually measure the constructs they claim to, we formalize the Cronbach--Meehl nomological network \cite{cronbach1955} as a \emph{partial-identification} problem \cite{manski2003} over a finite menu of candidate measures. The instrument is implemented in the open-source package \cvp{} (\texttt{SCORE} $\rightarrow$ \texttt{RESTRICT} $\rightarrow$ \texttt{IDENTIFY} $\rightarrow$ \texttt{REPORT}): given a researcher-authored nomological network of moment restrictions with literature-anchored thresholds, it computes slack profiles, the admissible set $\widehat{\mathcal{M}}^{*}$, and the construct-identified range $[\widehat{L},\widehat{U}]$ of any downstream target functional.

The methodological spine of the paper is a set of \emph{equivalences across literatures} that together formalize AI as an economic measurement instrument. First, \DPO{} training is Bradley--Terry maximum likelihood \cite{rafailov2023,bradley1952}: fine-tuning a policy on preference data is structural estimation of a random-utility model, so the trained policy's implied reward margins are utility differences. Second, option-likelihood scoring is Luce--Bradley--Terry--McFadden choice probability \cite{mcfadden1974}: the model's softmax over response options is a choice-probability simulator---a measurement function $m_{AI}(X)$ over the space of prompts and responses. Third, the Cronbach--Meehl nomological network is a system of moment inequalities \cite{cronbach1955,manski2003,dell2026}: validating a measure is computing an identified set---the same formal object at the center of the partial-identification literature, which converts ``which survey item should I use?'' from a judgment call into an identified set. None of these equivalences is new within its own literature; their \emph{fusion} is. The result is a general system of latent structural modeling with two components---a preference-alignment component (the pipeline) and a validation component (the measurement instrument)---of which cultural modeling is the first application. The SCA research agenda's fundamental objective is the \emph{measurement of culture}; this paper argues that the route runs through the general instrument, instantiated on culture, rather than through culture-specific engineering.

The empirical vehicle is the \GPS{}--\WVS{} bridge. The \GPS{} \cite{falk2018} supplies incentivized, behaviorally validated measures of patience, risk taking, positive and negative reciprocity, altruism, and trust across 76 countries. The World Values Survey supplies candidate survey measures of the same constructs. We evaluate (i)~whether the WVS facets are admissible representations of the GPS dimensions, at country level and at demographic-structure level; (ii)~whether DPO adapters trained on GPS-grounded synthetic data reproduce the empirical response distributions of their target countries on unseen WVS items, against a three-tier pre-registered ladder (synthetic recovery, survey out-of-sample, held-out and experimental validation); and (iii)~whether the choice of representation changes downstream conclusions---it does.

The paper proceeds as follows. Section~2 reviews the three literatures the framework spans. Section~3 presents the methods: the estimation pipeline and the measurement framework, with the formal specification of the network and a worked applied example on trust. Section~4 reports the empirical results across both tiers and the construct-validity layer. Section~5 discusses reliable and unreliable applications, frontier applications, and the broader positioning of rigorous measurement in developing economics. Section~6 outlines next steps.

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{LLMs as simulated subjects and synthetic populations}

A growing literature uses LLMs as simulated respondents \cite{horton2023,achar2023,argyle2023}; much of it is reduced-form role prompting, the family to which \SCA{}~1.0 belongs \cite{capra2025}. Complementary work documents cultural gaps and representation asymmetries in model outputs \cite{cao2023,durmus2024} but typically stops at evaluation rather than estimation. Recent econometric treatments of LLM measurement \cite{ludwig2026,carlsondell2025} frame model outputs as variables subject to measurement error and missing-data problems, reviving the validation-sample machinery of \cite{bound1991}. Our pipeline contributes the estimation side: rather than treating the model as an instrument to be corrected, we \emph{train} the cultural state into the policy, then validate the policy against human data.

\subsection{Preference estimation with LLMs}

\DPO{} \cite{rafailov2023} optimizes a policy so preferred responses receive higher probability than dispreferred ones relative to a reference model; under standard assumptions this coincides with Bradley--Terry maximum likelihood for a scaled log-probability score \cite{bradley1952,mcfadden1974}. We use this equivalence as a license to speak econometrically: the trained policy's implied reward margins are utility differences under the country-specific cultural state, identified up to scale and location. This structural reading of DPO is the bridge between the machine-learning pipeline and the measurement framework.

\subsection{Construct validity and the nomological network (psychometrics)}

Validity was classically defined by correlation with a criterion or by content judgment; both fail when the attribute is latent and no criterion exists. Cronbach and Meehl \cite{cronbach1955} resolved the crisis by introducing the \emph{nomological network}: a system of theoretical laws linking a construct to other constructs and to observables, so that validity becomes a matter of testing the network's implied relations. Campbell and Fiske \cite{campbell1959} supplied the operational template---convergent validity (measures of the same trait, ideally by different methods, cohere) and discriminant validity (measures of different traits do not collapse). Messick \cite{messick1995} unified the aspects of validity around score interpretation; Borsboom, Mellenbergh and van Heerden \cite{borsboom2004} added the crucial realism caveat: a network establishes \emph{consistency}, not \emph{causation}---the construct's existence and the direction of causation between attribute and score are not established by passing the network's restrictions.

\subsection{Partial identification and measurement in econometrics}

Leamer's extreme-bounds analysis \cite{leamer1983} asked how much conclusions move across specifications; Sala-I-Martin's ``two million regressions'' \cite{sala1997} made the question unavoidable once computation became cheap. Manski \cite{manski2003,manski2007} formalized the discipline: report the \emph{set} of parameter values consistent with the data and the stated assumptions---no more, no less. The econometric measurement literature supplies the treatment of mismeasured variables and multi-measure systems \cite{bound1991,schennach2021}, and the recent LLM-as-measurement framework of Ludwig, Mullainathan and Rambachan \cite{ludwig2026} connects these tools to frontier models. Dell and Rambachan's 2026 NBER Methods Lecture \cite{dell2026} argues that AI makes candidate measures cheap and shifts the bottleneck to \emph{choosing among many plausible representations}, which may yield different estimates of the target parameter; they propose formalizing the Cronbach--Meehl network as a partial-identification problem---the approach implemented here. What \cvp{} adds to that proposal is operationalization: a score-agnostic, model-free engine that binds a researcher-authored network to frozen score tables, computes slacks and admissible sets with run-id and hash discipline, and emits audit artifacts---so that the proposal becomes a reproducible procedure rather than a research agenda.

\subsection{The synthesis: construct validity as partial identification}

The two literatures answer different questions with different machinery, but they are the same question at different levels. Construct validity asks: \emph{which measures are admissible representations of the construct, given theory?} Partial identification asks: \emph{what can be learned about the target parameter, given data and assumptions?} A nomological network is a set of assumptions; its restrictions are moment inequalities; the admissible set $M^*$ is the identified set for the ``measure'' object; and the image of a downstream target $\beta$ on $M^*$ is the identified set for the conclusion. Our contribution at the methodological level is to make this equivalence operational in a reproducible open-source instrument (\cvp{}) and to demonstrate it on a real latent construct---country-level economic preference disposition---where the menu of candidate measures comes from two independent survey traditions.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{The synthetic-agent pipeline: synthetic experiments on cultural states}

\subsubsection{Primitives.}
Let $x$ denote a decision scenario (prompt) and $y$ a free-text response. A cultural state is a vector
\begin{equation}
  z = \bigl(\delta,\ \gamma,\ \xi^{+},\ \xi^{-},\ \alpha,\ \tau\bigr)^{\!\top} \in \mathbb{R}^{6},
\end{equation}
corresponding to time preference (patience), risk taking, positive reciprocity, negative reciprocity, altruism, and trust from the \GPS{}. An \SCA{} is a policy $\pi(\cdot\mid x; z)$ whose behavior is governed by a separable reward
\begin{equation}
  R(x,y;z) \;=\; r_{\mathrm{task}}(x,y) \;+\; \phi(z)^{\!\top} m(x,y),
  \label{eq:reward}
\end{equation}
where $r_{\mathrm{task}}$ captures generic competence (coherence, relevance), $m(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{6}$ is a vector of measurable behavioral features of the response, and $\phi:\mathbb{R}^{6}\to\mathbb{R}^{6}$ maps \GPS{} scores into reward weights.

\paragraph{Operationalization of the GPS state.}
The pipeline reads $z_c$ directly from the \GPS{} country file as \emph{standardized deviations from the global mean} (Falk et al.\ convention; one z-score per dimension per country), so $z_c$ is interpretable as ``country $c$ sits $z$ standard deviations above or below the global mean on dimension $d$.'' This choice is not incidental: it makes the six dimensions commensurable, pins down the sign of each score, and gives $\phi(z)$ the concrete reading of a map from ``SDs from the global mean'' into reward weights. The synthetic labels inherit this convention: in stage C, for each scenario the sign of $z_c$ on the target dimension selects which of the two fixed behavioral options is labeled as preferred (option $A$ when $z_c \ge 0$, option $B$ otherwise), so the training signal is a directional statement about the country's position relative to the world distribution, never about the country itself. Two interpretive consequences follow. First, the adapters are trained toward a \emph{\GPS{}-instrument-measured} construct; Tier-2 alignment is with that proxy, not with ``Mexican preferences'' in the abstract. Second, because the label rule is sign-based, the pipeline is insensitive to the magnitude of $z_c$ within a sign class---magnitude information enters only through the quality-control scores, not through the labels.

\paragraph{The structural assumption.}
Equation~\eqref{eq:reward} is a \emph{structural working assumption} (not an estimated fact): it claims that the country-specific component of an agent's behavior is a linear function of six externally measured preference traits, mediated by response-scoring features. Two testable implications follow. First, \emph{separability}: the task term $r_{\mathrm{task}}$ is country-invariant, so any cross-country behavioral difference must load on $\phi(z)^{\!\top} m(x,y)$. Second, \emph{dimensional structure}: only six dimensions of cultural variation are permitted to matter. Both implications are falsifiable---the first by the matched-versus-cross evaluation design (Section~4), the second by out-of-sample items that ``should not'' be explained by any dimension.

\subsubsection{Choice model.}
We treat the agent's response as a discrete choice over a finite option set $\mathcal{Y} = \{y_1,\dots,y_K\}$ (the response codes of a survey item or the two completions of a preference pair). A Luce--Bradley--Terry--McFadden random-utility model sets
\begin{equation}
  \Pr(y_k \mid x; z) \;=\; \frac{\exp\bigl(R(x,y_k;z)\bigr)}{\sum_{j=1}^{K}\exp\bigl(R(x,y_j;z)\bigr)},
  \label{eq:choice}
\end{equation}
which is the softmax over option rewards. For a pair $\{y^{w}, y^{\ell}\}$, equation~\eqref{eq:choice} implies the logistic paired-comparison probability
\begin{equation}
  \Pr(y^{w}\succ y^{\ell}\mid x) \;=\; \sigma\bigl(R(x,y^{w};z)-R(x,y^{\ell};z)\bigr),
  \label{eq:bt}
\end{equation}
with $\sigma(u) = (1+e^{-u})^{-1}$. Equation~\eqref{eq:choice} is the idealized object approximated by the evaluation pipeline: the option-likelihood protocol (Section~4) computes $\hat{p}_k \propto \exp(\text{logprob}(y_k\mid x))$ over all valid response codes and compares the resulting distribution to the empirical population distribution. The LLM is therefore evaluated as a \emph{choice-probability simulator}, not treated as a calibrated text generator.

\subsubsection{DPO as structural estimation.}
Given preference data $\{(x_i,y_i^{w},y_i^{\ell})\}_{i=1}^{n}$ in which $y^{w}$ is preferred to $y^{\ell}$ under cultural state $z$, the Bradley--Terry likelihood is
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}(R) \;=\; \prod_{i=1}^{n} \sigma\bigl(R(x_i,y_i^{w};z)-R(x_i,y_i^{\ell};z)\bigr).
  \label{eq:likelihood}
\end{equation}
Under the Bradley--Terry and KL-regularized-RL assumptions used to derive DPO, Direct Preference Optimization \cite{rafailov2023} has this likelihood form while reparameterizing reward differences through the log-ratio of the trained policy to a frozen reference policy:
\begin{equation}
  R(x,y_1;z)-R(x,y_2;z) \;=\; \beta\left[
    \log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_1\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_1\mid x)}
    -
    \log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_2\mid x)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y_2\mid x)}
  \right],
  \label{eq:logratio}
\end{equation}
where $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ is a frozen reference model and $\beta>0$ is a scale temperature. Equation~\eqref{eq:logratio} supplies an empirical reward-margin proxy for the structural reward in equation~\eqref{eq:reward}: after training, the analyst can compute, for any held-out pair, the \emph{implied reward margin} $\widehat{\Delta r} = \beta\,[\text{log-ratio of chosen} - \text{log-ratio of rejected}]$. We interpret this as a model-implied utility difference only under the maintained assumptions; it is not a separately identified human utility.

\paragraph{Identification and its limits.}
Equation~\eqref{eq:logratio} identifies reward \emph{differences} up to scale ($\beta$) and location. The two structural features we \emph{can} recover are (i)~the sign of $\Delta r$ and (ii)~the own-versus-other contrast: whether an adapter trained on country $c$'s labels assigns systematically larger margins to $c$'s chosen options than an adapter trained on $c'$. We cannot recover the absolute level of $R$ nor, in general, the individual coordinates of $\phi(z)$ without additional moment conditions.

\subsubsection{Pipeline stages.}
Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline} summarizes the system in five components: (1)~generation reads the cultural state $z_c$ (z-scores) from the \GPS{} country vector and produces country-neutral scenarios; (2)~cleanup and labeling score each pair on the six dimensions (monotonicity, distance, contamination) and attach sign-based labels; (3)~a student model is fine-tuned via QLoRA with unconditioned prompts---the country is never named; (4)~evaluation runs the two executed tiers (Tier-1 implied $\Delta r$ on held-out synthetic pairs; Tier-2 out-of-sample WVS option likelihood) plus the planned Tier-3; and (5)~the construct-validity layer of Section~3.2 admits the adapter as a candidate measure and returns the admissible set and identified range. Dashed arrows mark the planned feedback loops: validation rejection feeding new labels, and Tier-2 calibration feeding the training step.

\subsection{The measurement framework: construct validity as partial identification}

\subsubsection{The problem.}
The pipeline above produces agents whose behavior we interpret as a model-implied representation of country $c$'s preference state. Every link in that interpretation passes through \emph{measurement}: the \GPS{} state is a measure; the WVS items are measures; the adapter's option distributions are measures. None of them is the latent attribute itself. The scientific question---\emph{which of these candidate measures are admissible representations of the construct?}---is precisely the question the construct-validity tradition addresses, and we answer it with partial-identification machinery.

\subsubsection{Formal specification.}
Let $X$ denote the underlying reality (respondents, countries, their behaviors) and let a \emph{measurement function} $m_j(X)$ be any disciplined projection of $X$ into a score. The researcher supplies:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.5em]
  \item a \textbf{menu} $\mathcal{M} = \{m_j(\cdot)\}_{j \in \mathcal{J}}$ of candidate measures, already normalized so that both measures and downstream estimands are comparable across candidates;
  \item a \textbf{nomological network}---a set $\mathcal{R}$ of moment restrictions, each stating a required behavior of any valid measure:
  \begin{equation}
    r \in \mathcal{R}: \qquad \mathbb{E}\bigl[g_r(m(X), V; \theta_r)\bigr] \ge 0,
    \label{eq:restriction}
  \end{equation}
  where $V = (Z, W, G, Q)$ collects the auxiliary observables implicated by the network: $Z$ = related quantities (convergent evidence), $W$ = distinct quantities (discriminant evidence), $G$ = groups that should differ on the construct (known-groups evidence), $Q$ = conditioning covariates; and $\theta_r$ is the restriction's threshold, \emph{anchored in published evidence} rather than data-mined;
  \item a \textbf{tolerance} $\delta \ge 0$ (default $0$): a measure is admitted when all slacks satisfy $s_r(m) \ge -\delta$.
\end{enumerate}

The \emph{slack} of measure $m$ on restriction $r$ is the sample analogue of the left-hand side of equation~\eqref{eq:restriction}:
\begin{equation}
  s_r(m; \theta_r) \;=\; \widehat{\mathbb{E}}\bigl[g_r(m(X), V; \theta_r)\bigr],
\end{equation}
with positive values indicating satisfied restrictions and negative values the magnitude of violation. The \emph{admissible set} is
\begin{equation}
  \widehat{\mathcal{M}}^{*}_{\mathcal{R},\theta} \;=\; \left\{ m \in \mathcal{M} : s_r(m;\theta_r) \ge -\delta \ \ \forall r \in \mathcal{R} \right\}.
  \label{eq:admissible}
\end{equation}
This sample analogue encodes \emph{conceptual} uncertainty conditional on the observed data and researcher-specified restrictions. It is a deterministic screening rule unless sampling uncertainty is propagated, as described below. A population identified set would replace the sample slacks with their population counterparts; neither object is exhaustive unless the candidate menu and restrictions are justified as sufficiently rich.

For a downstream target functional $\beta(\cdot)$ (a regression coefficient, a correlation, a treatment effect defined using the measure), the \emph{construct-identified set} is
\begin{equation}
  \widehat{\mathcal{B}}^{*}_{\mathcal{R},\theta} \;=\; \left\{ \beta(m) : m \in \widehat{\mathcal{M}}^{*}_{\mathcal{R},\theta} \right\},
  \label{eq:bset}
\end{equation}
and, for scalar targets, the headline report is the \emph{construct-identified range}
\begin{equation}
  [\widehat{L}, \widehat{U}] \;=\; \left[ \inf_{m \in \widehat{\mathcal{M}}^{*}} \beta(m),\; \sup_{m \in \widehat{\mathcal{M}}^{*}} \beta(m) \right].
  \label{eq:range}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Inference and reproducibility.}
Sampling uncertainty is handled by bootstrap \emph{over units} (the menu is fixed, not resampled), reporting percentile bands on the range endpoints over non-empty replicates; degenerate replicates are counted and reported. Threshold sensitivity is reported as a multiplicative $\lambda$-grid diagnostic (the headline is $\lambda=1$; grid settings are excluded from the run identity). Paper-relevant numbers require frozen scores, pinned network/$\beta$, fixed seed, and package version, with a deterministic run id derived from their hash. Empty admissible sets and wide ranges are valid scientific outputs; thresholds are never loosened to avoid them.

\paragraph{Implementation.}
The framework is implemented in the open-source package \cvp{} \cite{cvprofiles} (\url{github.com/Bonorinoa/cvprofiles}), whose state machine is exactly \texttt{SCORE} $\rightarrow$ \texttt{RESTRICT} $\rightarrow$ \texttt{IDENTIFY} $\rightarrow$ \texttt{REPORT}: it ingests a frozen unit$\times$measure score table, binds the researcher-authored network and target functional, computes slacks and the admissible set, and emits JSON/HTML audit artifacts with run-id and hash discipline. The engine is score-agnostic and model-free; it contains no LLM calls. The network is always researcher-authored; the tool only evaluates.

\section{Empirical Results}

\subsection{Evaluation methodology}

All adapters are evaluated frozen, under unconditioned prompts (\texttt{PROMPT\_COUNTRY\_CONDITIONING = False}), so the target country is never named at inference and every difference between adapters must be carried by the weights alone. The primary comparison is \emph{matched versus cross}: an adapter trained on country $c$'s labels is compared, item by item, with an adapter trained on country $c'$'s labels, against the external human criterion of survey-weighted WVS response distributions $P_{\mathrm{WVS}}$. Because the criterion is human survey data, not another model, the design answers the correlated-errors objection to validating AI measures against AI measures.

The evaluation follows a pre-registered three-tier ladder (\texttt{WVS\_ITEM\_MAP}, committed before training): \textbf{Tier~1} checks whether each adapter recovers its own culture-conditioned labels on held-out synthetic pairs (an in-sample sanity check); \textbf{Tier~2} tests out-of-sample generalization to $N=35$ unseen Wave~7 WVS items (30 mapped to GPS dimensions, 5 demographic) against human response distributions; \textbf{Tier~3} reserves held-out items and behavioral tasks (e.g., economic games) that are never used in training or testing---these are planned but not yet executed. Table~\ref{tab:evidence} summarizes the key evidence by tier; the rest of the section reports each tier in turn.

\paragraph{Key Finding 1: Distributional Alignment on Non-WEIRD Targets.}
On Mexico WVS data, the matched MEX adapter achieves strong distributional alignment, outperforming the cross USA adapter on $97.1\%$ of questions ($34/35$ items). The mean difference in Total Variation Distance ($\text{TVD}_{\text{cross}} - \text{TVD}_{\text{matched}}$) is $+0.1495$ ($95\%$ CI: $[0.0964, 0.2152]$). Because prompts omit country names, this difference can only arise from the adapter weights: each adapter emits a single fixed response distribution, and the MEX adapter's fixed distribution sits closer to Mexico's observed WVS responses than the USA adapter's. This is evidence that country-specific dispositional signal was internalized into the weights during fine-tuning; it does not demonstrate that an adapter conditions on or ``knows'' its target country at inference time.

\emph{Plain-language reading.} If the adapter weights were doing nothing, the matched and cross adapters would perform equally well and the split would be roughly $50/50$; a win rate of $34/35$ is far from that benchmark, with a confidence interval that does not cross zero. The result is directional, not calibrated: we do not claim that the adapter predicts the percentage of Mexicans who trust their family, only that it ranks trust patterns in the direction the empirical distribution supports.

\paragraph{Key Finding 2: USA Adapter Degradation (mechanism unresolved).}
On USA WVS data, the matched USA adapter performs \emph{worse} than both the base model ($\text{TVD} = 0.5258$ vs.\ $0.3958$) and the cross MEX adapter ($\text{TVD} = 0.3888$). Only $11.4\%$ of questions favored the matched USA adapter. The degradation is not confined to US data: the USA adapter also worsens fit on Mexico's items ($\text{TVD} = 0.6377$ vs.\ base $0.5211$). The mechanism---for example, a collision with the base model's strong American pretraining prior, versus overfitting to the synthetic scenario format---is not identified by this design.

\emph{Plain-language reading.} ``Pretraining prior'' refers to the implicit population the model assumes when asked an English-language survey question without country context---a population that looks American. Adding synthetic US signal on top of that prior moves the policy away from the empirical US WVS distribution (TVD $0.396 \rightarrow 0.526$); the USA adapter also moves away from Mexico's distribution ($0.521 \rightarrow 0.638$), so the degradation is not specific to US items. The asymmetry with Mexico is consistent with the prior-collision account, but competing explanations remain viable; the mechanism should be treated as unresolved.

\paragraph{Key Finding 3: Structural Under-Dispersion.}
Across all models, option log-likelihood scoring suffers from severe \textbf{under-dispersion} (dispersion bias ranging from $-1.15$ to $-1.63$). Softmax normalization concentrates probability mass on modal choices, underestimating real-world human population variance. Any claim about \emph{absolute shares} (``$42\%$ of Mexicans answer X'') is unsupported by the current pipeline; claims about \emph{direction and rank} are.

\subsection{Construct-validity results}

The measurement framework of Section~3.2 answers three questions, and the results below are organized accordingly. \emph{First}, \textbf{admissibility}: which candidate measures survive the nomological network, and which fail? \emph{Second}, \textbf{consequentiality}: does the choice among admissible measures change downstream conclusions, or is representation choice cosmetic? \emph{Third}, \textbf{structure}: do the surviving measures reproduce the GPS benchmark's demographic patterns, at gradient and cell level?

\paragraph{Admissibility of WVS facets as GPS representations.}
Table~\ref{tab:typology} is the headline: only 5 of 16 candidate facets are admissible representations of the six GPS dimensions at the literature-anchored threshold. The failures are \emph{informative}: risktaking's mapped economic-values items do not behave like GPS risk preferences at any resolution (empty $M^*$ even at $\lambda=0.5$), and the age-shape diagnostic catches financial satisfaction (Q50) as a U-shaped rather than hump-shaped patience proxy---admissible at country level, structurally wrong at individual level.

\paragraph{Instrument equivalence: Q57 vs.\ the survivor composite.}
The single-item trust measure and the multi-item composite agree on \emph{average} trust across countries but disagree on \emph{who} has it. At country level, Q57 correlates $0.28$ with GPS trust versus $0.41$ for the composite. Across 42 countries, the cross-country correlation of the WVS-vs-GPS \emph{gender gaps} is $0.005$ for Q57 (no structural alignment) versus $0.568$ for the composite (country-cluster bootstrap, $P(\text{composite} > \text{Q57}) = 0.999$). At cell level (sex$\times$age cells within countries), Q57's pooled within-country correlation with GPS trust is $+0.087$ (CI crosses zero) versus $+0.334$ for the composite (CI $[0.234, 0.415]$; robust to GPS weights). The economic-model implication: any model that interacts trust with demographic composition will obtain GPS-inconsistent trust effects when fed Q57---the canonical item used across the empirical trust literature.

\paragraph{Demographic-gradient structure (Stream B).}
The WVS facets reproduce GPS's positive education/cognitive gradient for the trust facets, patience-financial, and negrecip-justifiability (pooled $z$ between $+4.8$ and $+11.1$), but largely fail GPS's gender gradient---most sharply for trust-general and out-group, where the WVS female coefficient is significantly \emph{negative} where GPS trust is positive. This is direct evidence that GPS trust and WVS generalized trust are related but not interchangeable measures, and it pre-registers which channels the adapter evaluation must reproduce.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{What the framework establishes}

The two contributions answer different questions, and both answers are needed. The pipeline establishes that a measured cultural state can be encoded into a policy such that the resulting fixed response distribution aligns with an external human criterion for populations under-represented in pretraining---an \emph{estimation} result. The measurement framework establishes which survey measures are admissible representations of the incentivized constructs---a \emph{validity} result, with the proviso, following Borsboom et al. \cite{borsboom2004}, that passing a network's restrictions establishes consistency with the network, not causation between attribute and score. The \cvp{} instrument makes the validity result reproducible: thresholds anchored in published evidence, slacks reported in full, empty sets reported as findings, and run identities derived from frozen inputs.

\subsection{Reliable and unreliable applications}

\paragraph{Reliable.}
(i)~\textbf{Distributional fidelity shifts}: option-choice probabilities move toward empirical population distributions for populations under-represented in pretraining, measured without country prompts---the adapter's \emph{fixed} distribution is closer to Mexico's observed responses. (ii)~\textbf{Relative/ordinal comparative statics}: tracking the \emph{direction} of change in a preference dimension across populations, instruments, or experimental arms, not absolute shares. (iii)~\textbf{Pre-field instrument stress-testing}: probing draft questionnaire items against a target population benchmark before expensive fieldwork; items where model distributions diverge sharply from the benchmark are flagged for translation or scale artifacts. (iv)~\textbf{Measurement-fragility quantification}: the construct-identified range $[L,U]$ tells the analyst how much of a downstream conclusion is attributable to measurement choice---the H5 example bounds the trust--development correlation between $0.371$ and $0.624$ across admissible measures.

\paragraph{Unreliable.}
(i)~\textbf{Absolute headcount forecasting and polling}: structural under-dispersion makes percentage-share predictions unsupported. (ii)~\textbf{Fine-tuning over already-dominant priors}: the measured degradation of the USA adapter suggests the right reaction may be to leave the base model alone or use an anchoring regularizer---a hypothesis for the next iteration, not an established conclusion. (iii)~\textbf{Free-form conversational persona chat}: option-likelihood validation does not certify open-ended dialogue. (iv)~\textbf{Granular trait elasticities on thin cells}: per-dimension per-question estimates on small cells carry wide confidence intervals and should not be quoted as calibrated structural parameters.

\subsection{Frontier applications}

The framework opens applications beyond the current pipeline's verified surface; we list them to scope the program.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.5em]
  \item \textbf{Pre-field design loops: power analysis without priors and survey-item pre-piloting.} The construct-identified range $[L,U]$ is a set of plausible effect sizes across admissible measures, so a researcher can compute power curves across the whole range instead of committing to a single prior---an honest way to size studies when measurement choice is contested. The same pipeline probes draft items through validated adapters before fielding: items whose distributions diverge sharply from the target benchmark, or whose implied facet scores violate the network's restrictions, are flagged pre-field. Survey design becomes a falsifiable loop rather than expert judgment.
  \item \textbf{Inducing a preference construct in an arbitrary LLM.} Because the cultural state enters only through the training labels, the pipeline can induce \emph{any} GPS-parameterizable disposition in \emph{any} open-weight base model: given a target state $z^*$, generate pairs under $z^*$, fine-tune, and validate with the same tiered protocol. This generalizes from ``culture'' to arbitrary preference engineering---e.g., inducing a specified risk attitude in a model that otherwise has none.
  \item \textbf{Activation-steered constructs (derivative work).} Steering vectors \cite{turner2023,zou2023}---directions in activation space that modulate behavior at inference time---provide a \emph{no-training} route to construct induction: a steering vector for a preference dimension yields a policy whose option distributions are a new measurement function $m_{\mathrm{steer}}(X)$, a candidate in the same menu as $m_{\mathrm{base}}$ and $m_{\mathrm{DPO}}$. Because steering intensity is continuous, the menu becomes a continuum and admissibility a function of steering strength; where a steered measure crosses into the admissible set is a framework-answerable question. This line is planned as a derivative project aimed at a methods/CS venue, not part of the current empirical claims.
  \item \textbf{A validation feedback loop and a community construct registry.} If the validation protocol works, the network's restrictions become training objectives: fine-tuning can be gated on whether the implied measures satisfy the moment inequalities, yielding \emph{theoretically-aligned} models---policies trained to satisfy a stated nomological network. A public registry where research groups contribute construct definitions (score matrices plus nomological networks, subject to anchored thresholds and frozen runs) would let measurement validity accumulate as a community resource rather than per-paper judgment.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Positioning: measurement and developing economics}

The field's standard response to latent constructs is to \emph{justify a single proxy} and proceed. That response is increasingly untenable: the canonical trust item and a multi-item composite disagree on \emph{who} trusts, and the choice between them moves the trust--development correlation by a quarter of a correlation point. AI collapses the cost of candidate measures; partial identification over a nomological network supplies the discipline; the two together give developing economics a reproducible procedure for saying which measures are admissible and what conclusions survive all admissible ones. The implications run from hypothesis generation (survey new items before fielding) to cross-country policy design (which representation drives the policy conclusion---and what set of conclusions is robust to representation choice).

\subsection{Limitations}

The Tier-2 evaluation compares adapter outputs to human survey responses---an external, non-AI criterion---which answers the correlated-errors objection to validating AI measures against AI measures \cite{dell2026}. Three caveats remain. First, the adapters and their synthetic training labels share a common generative ancestry, so Tier-1 self-recovery is in-sample evidence only. Second, the mechanism behind the USA adapter's degradation is unresolved. Third, the country panel is two adapters and two evaluation countries; the measurement layer runs on 42 countries, but the adapter layer does not. The ecological caveat applies throughout: country-level correlations are development-confounded, which is why the demographic-gradient and cell-level resolutions are load-bearing rather than decorative.

\section{Next Steps}

The agenda below is the minimal credible next iteration. \textbf{Stream A (measurement):} fold the P1, demographic-gradient, and cell-mean layers into single multi-restriction \cvp{} networks per dimension (adding discriminant and known-groups restrictions), and adopt a downstream target $\beta$ (e.g., log-GDP correlation) so that every dimension reports a construct-identified range. \textbf{Stream B (persona gradients):} run the frozen pre-registered persona-conditioned evaluation---the designed adjudication of whether the adapters encode demographic structure---with the survivor composite as the WVS-side reference. \textbf{Stream C (falsification):} add a third country (Argentina) whose GPS profile differs from Mexico's to test the prior-collision account, growing the adapter panel at the current training-data scale---intentionally capped to avoid new dataset generation; and reframe the AmericasBarometer surface as an instrument-redundancy check (its local coverage is USA/MEX only). Adapter \emph{mixtures} (convex combinations of adapter weights or output distributions) are themselves candidate measures: the validity layer can test whether intermediate GPS states are admissible, treating interpolation as a measurement-construction device. \textbf{Stream D (calibration):} address under-dispersion via per-question calibration and temperature sensitivity. \textbf{Stream E (provenance):} reproduce and commit the Tier-1 cross-evaluation outputs.

\section{Worked example: the age-shape restriction on patience}

\label{app:patience}

The trust profile (Section~3.3) illustrates the framework with a single convergent restriction. The patience dimension illustrates a second, structurally different restriction: a \emph{known-groups} style condition on the shape of a demographic profile. GPS patience is hump-shaped in age---rising into midlife and declining after---with an estimated age coefficient of $+0.72$ and age-squared of $-1.45$ in the pooled GPS individual data (Table~\ref{tab:ageshape}, left column). The WVS candidate that passes the country-level convergent restriction, financial satisfaction (Q50), has the \emph{opposite} curvature: U-shaped, with age-squared $+1.41$ ($z = +7.3$, USA sample), i.e., financial satisfaction rises with age. A country-level correlation of $r = 0.43$ between Q50 and GPS patience passes a convergent threshold, yet the individual-level age structure fails the shape restriction outright.

A measure that passes country-level convergence but fails the shape restriction is admissible at the moment level and structurally wrong at the individual level.}
\label{tab:ageshape}

The example makes the framework's discriminations visible: the country-level correlation alone would admit Q50; the age-shape restriction does not. This is the same machinery that rejects the canonical trust item---a single convergent threshold is a weak filter, and the network's power comes from stacking restrictions of different types (convergent, discriminant, known-groups) on the same menu.
