\section{Synthetic Respondents as Measurement Instruments}\label{sec:background}

\paragraph{Synthetic respondents and their critics.} The proposal that a language model conditioned on a description of a population can stand in for a sample of its members has two well-known forms. In silicon sampling, the description enters the prompt and the model's answer distribution is read as an estimate of the population's \citep{argyle2023}; in the homo silicus program, the model plays an economic agent in a stylized decision problem \citep{horton2023}. A systematic review maps the rapid spread of these uses across survey research \citep{buskirk2026}. Where prompted models have been compared with human surveys, their answers are sensitive to prompt wording and unstable over time \citep{bisbee2024}, and they show a strong bias and low variance on each topic, with a bias that changes unpredictably from one topic to the next \citep{boelaert2025}. Prompting with group labels can also flatten and misportray the groups it names \citep{wang2025}. Fine-tuning on survey responses is one answer to these problems \citep{suh2025,cao2025,krsteski2026}, and the choice between aligned and unaligned models changes how faithfully a model simulates population attitudes \citep{lyman2025}. Two reviews in this journal draw the methodological conclusion that matters here. \citet{kozlowski2025} identify features of current models that impair the realistic simulation of human subjects and conclude that validation against human data remains essential. \citet{broska2025} propose a mixed subjects design in which model predictions are potentially informative observations and human subjects remain the gold standard. Both positions require a criterion against which the synthetic reading can fail.

\paragraph{Construct, measure, and anchor.} Synthetic respondents are easier to evaluate when three objects are kept apart. A construct is a theoretical preference attribute, such as generalized trust. A measure operationalizes it through observed answers or choices, such as the response distribution to a WVS question about whether most people can be trusted. An anchor is an aggregate score that conditions the instrument, such as a country's GPS trust coordinate. The GPS supplies such anchors for six economic preferences (trust, risk taking, patience, altruism, positive reciprocity and negative reciprocity), measured with experimentally validated survey modules in a common design across countries \citep{falk2018}. We treat these coordinates as declared aggregate moments: the researcher chooses them, and nothing about the instrument's construction certifies that they are the right moments for a given survey. Mapping a WVS item to a GPS dimension makes the item a candidate indicator of that dimension, and an average of such items is a proposed composite until its interpretation is supported. Agreement between an instrument and an anchor is therefore a different fact from agreement between an instrument and people, and agreement between a survey and an anchor across countries is a third. The multitrait--multimethod logic of \citet{campbellfiske1959} applies directly: the human survey is the other method, and an instrument that agrees with its own anchor has passed a construction check without yet meeting a criterion.

\paragraph{Cross-national comparability.} The unit of analysis in this paper is the country. Country-level agreement does not establish that an item measures the same construct for individuals in different countries, and the literature on measurement equivalence explains why: loadings and intercepts can differ across groups in ways that leave country means comparable for some purposes and misleading for others \citep{steenkamp1998,davidov2014}. Recent work in this journal argues for a pragmatic approach in which composite scores aggregated to the country level can suffice for simple group comparisons despite noninvariance, while warning that such scores can understate uncertainty \citep{bratt2025}, and shows by simulation that unaddressed measurement error and, to a lesser degree, noninvariance bias multilevel estimates \citep{pokropek2025}. We adopt the same posture. The screen in this paper asks a group-level directional question about country rankings, and it makes no claim about invariance or individual-level validity.

\paragraph{Pairwise choice and preference optimization.} The instrument rests on a correspondence between a training objective for language models and a classical model of paired comparisons. Under the \citet{bradleyterry1952} model, the probability that one option is chosen over another is a logistic function of the difference in their latent utilities, which is the binary logit of random-utility theory. Direct Preference Optimization fits a policy to labeled pairs by maximizing exactly this likelihood, with the utility index written as a scaled log ratio between the fitted policy and a fixed reference policy \citep{rafailov2023}. The scale parameter penalizes divergence from the reference, so the fitted policy is a regularized Bradley--Terry estimator. The synthetic labels in our application are deterministic; we use the likelihood as a fitting criterion and make no claim that it models human choice noise.

\paragraph{The empty cell.} Prior evaluations of synthetic respondents score models on questions whose human answers are known, usually with the population named in the prompt, and fine-tuning approaches learn from human survey responses. What is missing is a test of a fixed synthetic-respondent instrument as a screen: an instrument built from declared aggregate inputs alone, applied to items it was not built for, with its selection rule and inference registered before the human criterion is read, and compared with screens that read only the questionnaire. Section~\ref{sec:design} describes such a test. Whether the instrument passes it is a question for Section~\ref{sec:results}.
