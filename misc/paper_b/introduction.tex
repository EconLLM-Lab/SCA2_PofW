\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

Cross-national survey programs choose their items long before they know how those items will behave. A candidate question about trust in institutions or willingness to take risks must be translated, pretested, and fielded in many countries before a researcher can see whether its country-level differences line up with the construct it was meant to capture. Language models offer a tempting shortcut: a researcher supplies a question and a description of a population and reads off synthetic responses \citep{argyle2023,horton2023,buskirk2026}. The same literature documents why that shortcut is fragile. Synthetic responses are sensitive to prompt wording, compress the variance of human answers, and can misportray and flatten identity groups \citep{bisbee2024,boelaert2025,wang2025}, and the alignment procedures that make models usable change how faithfully they simulate population attitudes \citep{lyman2025}. Methodological work in this journal concludes that validation against human data remains essential, and proposes designs that treat model predictions as potentially informative observations beside a human gold standard \citep{kozlowski2025,broska2025}.

Most of that empirical work asks whether a model reproduces the answers people gave to questions whose answers were already known. Survey design poses a different question. Before fielding, the useful property of an instrument is whether it can tell a researcher which of many new items are likely to separate countries in the intended direction, when the instrument was fixed before those items existed. That is a screening problem, and a screening instrument has to be judged on items it was not built for, with a decision rule fixed in advance and a human criterion read only afterwards. It also has to be judged against cheaper alternatives, because a screen that does no better than a careful reading of the questionnaire adds cost without adding information.

We study one such instrument, which we call synthetic cultural agents: group-indexed choice policies built by compiling the signs of declared country-level preference benchmarks from the Global Preferences Survey \citep[GPS;][]{falk2018} into parameter adapters. Each country's sign profile deterministically labels a shared bank of paired synthetic responses, and Direct Preference Optimization \citep{rafailov2023}, read as a regularized Bradley--Terry pairwise choice model, fits one adapter per profile. The adapters are evaluated with prompts that never name a country. We developed the construction on a purposive panel of sixteen countries and evaluate it here on every country covered by both the GPS and Wave~7 of the World Values Survey (WVS). The construction is inspectable: the researcher controls which aggregate moments enter, the sign rule that turns them into labels, and whether the group is named at evaluation.

This paper asks what that construction is worth as a scoring instrument for new item text, under conditions designed to make the answer hard to flatter. We trained adapters for every country in the intersection of the GPS and WVS Wave~7 rather than a selected panel, and a second set of adapters for all \dc{64}{cells} possible sign profiles of the six GPS dimensions on a newly generated scenario bank. We then ran three evaluations. The first asks whether the trained surfaces of the original battery survive the replacement of the scenario bank. The second is a preregistered screen of \dc{223}{pool_items} WVS Wave~7 items that no adapter was built for: a probe that reads only the adapters' scores assigns each item a preference dimension and a direction, a fixed rule selects the items with the strongest assignments, and only then are the selected items compared with the human country differences. The third uses the full factorial of sign profiles as a diagnostic of whether the six declared dimensions behave as separate inputs, and compares the trained adapters with a prompt that states the same six signs.

The results are mixed, and we report them as they came out. Trust on the original battery carries over to the second scenario bank; for risk taking, positive reciprocity and altruism, the agreement with the GPS keeps a positive point estimate but its interval now includes zero. A prompt that names the country does not reproduce the GPS trust order, and on trust the adapters track that order more closely than the named-country prompt does. In the retrospective screen of held-out items, the probe-selected items predict the direction of human country differences better than random assignment of the same dimensions and signs, and the screen therefore rejects its preregistered null. The signal is weak: it is a small fraction of the agreement that two waves of the same human survey achieve on the same items, and its bootstrap interval reaches zero. Within the screen, only the items assigned to risk taking stand apart from zero, and that dimension does not survive an exploratory adjustment for the four dimensions tested; the items assigned to positive reciprocity point the wrong way. On the few items where they overlap, the registered contrasts do not distinguish the adapters from a careful reader of the questionnaire. In the factorial, every sign bit moves its own surface in the intended direction, but the bits also move each other's surfaces, often by more, and the planned check of separability fails. Prompting with the same signs is more entangled still and moves three surfaces the wrong way. No preference dimension passes both the second-bank check and the held-out screen.

Two features of the design bound what these results can say. The human criterion is retrospective: the WVS Wave~7 file existed before the probe, the item pool and the adapters, and it is the only human source. The screen therefore tests whether the instrument's selections agree with an existing survey; it cannot show that screening would change which items a researcher fields. And the instrument is built from signs alone, so it carries no information about magnitudes or about variation among people within a country.

Our contributions are three.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{The instrument at full scale.} We extend the sign compiler from a selected panel to every GPS--WVS country and to the complete factorial of sign profiles on a second scenario bank, so that the construction can be read as a designed manipulation of synthetic training labels.
\item \textbf{A preregistered retrospective screening protocol.} We specify, register, and run a screen of new survey items with a probe fixed before any human value was read, a permutation null that absorbs the probe's own selection rate, a human ceiling from two survey waves, and two comparators that read only the questionnaire.
\item \textbf{A diagnostic of entanglement.} We use the sign-profile factorial to show which declared dimensions the instrument keeps apart and which it confounds, and we compare trained adapters with prompting on the same information.
\end{enumerate}

Together these contributions describe what the sign compiler holds, where it fails, and what remains untested when it is used as a scoring instrument for item text. The intended use is group-level: a reusable, inspectable instrument that a survey designer can consult beside human pretesting, and whose readings concern differences among countries rather than the answers of any person. Section~\ref{sec:background} places the instrument among current uses of synthetic respondents and in the measurement literature on cross-national comparability. Section~\ref{sec:instrument} describes the construction, Section~\ref{sec:design} the evaluation design and its registration, Section~\ref{sec:results} the results, and Section~\ref{sec:discussion} what they permit a researcher to do.
