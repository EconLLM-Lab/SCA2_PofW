\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}
Group-level preference facts are expensive to obtain and easy to over-interpret. A proposed survey item must be piloted, translated, and fielded before researchers can assess whether it measures the intended attribute across populations. Language models offer a cheaper preliminary readout: researchers supply an item and a population description and obtain synthetic responses \cite{argyle2023,horton2023,buskirk2026}. Yet sensitivity to prompting, compressed variance, and unstable subgroup comparisons limit the interpretation of those responses \cite{bisbee2024,boelaert2025,wang2025}. In standard prompting pipelines, population conditioning remains opaque: responses inevitably confound the researcher's intended demographic cues with associations acquired during pretraining. A central methodological question is what population information the researcher explicitly supplies and what subsequent comparisons can establish.

We address this opacity by compiling declared aggregate preference signs into group-indexed parameter adapters. Specifically, we define a synthetic cultural agent as a group-indexed choice policy. It is neither an autonomous agent nor a sample of human respondents. A researcher declares aggregate preference benchmarks, takes their signs to deterministically label a shared bank of contrasting synthetic responses, and fits a parameter adapter for each population using Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) \cite{rafailov2023}. We interpret this loss through a regularized Bradley--Terry pairwise choice model. Each adapter is jointly trained across multiple preference dimensions. At evaluation, the fitted policy assigns probabilities to candidate response codes for new survey items using a country-name-free prompt. Omitting the country name from the evaluation prompt removes that prompting channel. Pretrained associations remain in the base model.

Our empirical application evaluates this construction by linking country scores from the Global Preferences Survey (GPS) \cite{falk2018} to candidate items in World Values Survey (WVS) Wave~7 \cite{wvs2022}. To assess what this pipeline establishes, we separate four distinct evaluation criteria: held-out synthetic-label recovery, cross-text anchor transfer, human cross-survey coherence, and model-human agreement. Internal recovery of synthetic labels confirms that optimization internalizes the training signs, whereas cross-text anchor transfer tests whether the coarse sign transfers to unseen survey items. Human cross-survey coherence evaluates whether human responses on the target instrument align with the external anchor, and model-human agreement measures direct concordance between policy scores and human survey distributions. These readouts target fundamentally different estimands: repeated language-model queries reduce Monte Carlo error around a functional of the model's conditional distribution, whereas larger human samples reduce sampling error around a population functional. Moreover, because the construction relies entirely on published aggregate scores without access to individual-level microdata, the sign vectors do not identify within-group preference distributions. Group-level correspondence across countries faces standard ecological limits, meaning that cross-national alignment cannot establish individual-level measurement validity.

In this application, internal recovery of synthetic labels is strong across all groups on held-out comparisons. On the trust dimension, the fitted policies transfer the coarse GPS sign to new item wording. The comparison with a country-named prompt is descriptive and is reported with the results. However, model-human agreement remains unresolved on this development panel, as does the human survey composite's own coherence with the anchor. Across the remaining preference dimensions, performance diverges: while trust demonstrates directional anchor transfer, other traits display weak or conflicting associations across criteria. Furthermore, prompting with country names yields response distributions closer to observed human choice distributions in total variation distance, demonstrating that distributional resemblance and directional anchor retention can favor different model configurations.

Our contributions are threefold. First, we provide a compiler that maps declared aggregate preference signs into parameter adapters, establishing an inspectable route from population benchmarks to choice policies evaluated without country names. Second, we introduce an evaluation framework that explicitly separates internal label recovery, cross-text anchor transfer, human cross-survey coherence, and model-human agreement. Third, we offer an empirical proof of concept demonstrating that these validation criteria can sharply disagree: a policy can successfully retain an external anchor without matching human response distributions, and prompting can mimic human response shapes without transferring the anchor sign. The intended application of this pipeline is group-level analysis and survey pre-piloting as a complement to field research. Whether using such policies for prospective item screening improves item selection before fielding a human survey remains an empirical hypothesis for a later human study.
